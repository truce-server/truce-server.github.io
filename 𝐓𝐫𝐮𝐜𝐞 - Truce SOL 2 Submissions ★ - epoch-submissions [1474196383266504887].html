Bitter, put simply, is a song about loss. More complicatedly, it is a song about loss, refusal of acceptance, abandonment, mistakes, and a grief so deep that it is not noticed until it is too late. Bitter is a song written for a relative of the band's lead singer, Jonathan Garrett, better known by his alias, "Johnny Manchild." This song is about a broken household written from the perspective of a child going through the divorce of their parents. In this divorce, he chooses to stay with his father who does not care for him. Despite his father's lack of love, he convinces himself that if he stays around, he'll earn it one day, as long as he stays "just one more night." The child doesn't realise that by choosing his father, he is putting his mother through more grief than she can imagine, with her having to "make it up" to him, the memories that will eventually become a bitter reminder of the wrong choices he made. The narrator of this song, likely the mother, sings that "you'll be bitter, not right away, but you'll understand one of these days," explaining that while the child may be 'happy' in his longing for his father, he will come to realise that his sorrow will never be resolved due to his father's neglect, and then it will be up to his mother to make it up to him. This track is sung beautifully, from gorgeous falsetto to soaring tones that carry more hope as the child tries to convince himself that it will all be ok, the final line of the track, "you'll make it out," fading slowly to symbolise the child struggling to believe that they will be ok coming out of this, the realisation breaking him as the words get further and further away. The instrumental changes throughout the track, from soft piano, to a more complex sound, right back to that soft piano sound. It's a faltering attempt to lash out that comprises that complex section, the child angry, blind to the love his mother gives in a pointless desperation that his father will be able to provide something that he is never willing to. In the end, this song is a tragedy, one that we know the ending of, a child living with the grief of knowing that they chose the wrong parent, endlessly trying to make it up to his mother. # MUSE: Stockholm Syndrome

This song, I think, perfectly encapsulates Muse in their entirety, their sound, their lyrics, and their art. The instrumentals are incredible, the opening drums are some of the most fun and bouncy I've heard in a song, the guitar solo is a signature of muse and they absolutely knock this one out of the park, and the vocals, my god the vocals. Matt Bellamy puts his true talents on display as one of the best vocalists of his time, showing off his entire range of soft falsetto to raging screams, the array of sounds that describe Muse to a T. Not only was this off of their first hit album, propelling them to stardom, but it's a song that sounds special, no matter the context, and no matter who is listening. That is why this is Muse's magnum opus.

# JOHNNY MANCHILD AND THE POOR BASTARDS: Rapture Waltz
Epoch [S1 - 14th] 2026-03-24 10:29 PM
It's hard to describe how special this song is. It's special in general, it's special to me, it's special to the band. This choice had me conflicted for a very long time, but Rapture Waltz is, in my opinion, Johnny Manchild and the Poor Bastard's magnum opus. The eponymous track of the album Rapture Waltz, this track ends out the band's best album yet with a ballad dedicated to the feeling of the world leaving you behind. It constantly builds throughout the entire song, the sound of the rapture starting, the beginning of the end, your world no longer makes sense to you as things get scrambled into senseless, meaningless nothingness. And then comes the drop, the best drop in any song they have ever produced. The already phenomenal piano is layered with a gorgeous synth, both played by the band's lead singer Johnny Manchild, creating a rolling feeling that is almost euphoric, while the guitar and drums remain behind, slower, an incredible juxtaposition which just makes the moment feel right. And then it's gone. In one fell swoop, everything you knew is gone other than the piano, representing the singer and the listener, only echoes of the other instruments remain as you remember and cling onto your life before. It has a finality to it, the sound of an ending happening that you weren't ready for, and now you're stuck here after the credits. This song is their magnum opus because it's important to their sound, their songs have never been the same since because of Rapture Waltz's influence and they're all the better for it. Additionally, when this song came out, I was at an incredibly tumultuous period in my life. I was finishing school, leaving my friends behind, the routine I'd known for 13 years and I felt like I wasn't ready for it. This song helped me to describe those feelings, to feel like there was somebody else that understood, something that's so important because this is such a universal experience. This is why I think Rapture Waltz is their magnum opus, and why it's one of my favourite songs I've ever heard.
